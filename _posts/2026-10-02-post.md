I am sure you have probably heard the phrase <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China.”</span> You have probably also seen headlines about Chinese cars, electronics, luxury brands, or China’s role in rare earths. China makes and processes a huge amount of what the world uses, but I wanted to look at the physical geography behind that production.

Part of why I chose China is personal. I just came back from studying away in Shanghai, and I miss it. China is also a particularly interesting country to map because extraction, processing, manufacturing, transport, and trade all overlap across a huge and varied landscape.

China’s scale is hard to overstate. In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. These minerals are essential to semiconductors, batteries, electric vehicles, renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

China also dominates refining, leading in **19 of the 20 strategic minerals** tracked by the IEA, with an average global market share of around **70%**. [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary)

The same scale appears in manufacturing and shipping. In 2025, China accounted for **56.1% of global shipbuilding output**, while Chinese ports handled **18.3 billion tonnes of cargo** and **354 million TEUs**. [Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://www.miit.gov.cn/gxsj/tjfx/zbgy/mycb/art/2026/art_fc5e4ed1d3f5421885e6c3afff91cbbc.html) [State Council of China](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202606/30/content_WS6a43bc0fc6d00ca5f9a0bf02.html)

Without China, many global supply chains would look very different. Which brings me to my map: where is this activity actually happening?

## What I Decided to Map

I chose three GeoNames feature codes that capture different parts of China’s production landscape:

| Feature Code | Feature | GeoNames definition |
|:---:|---|---|
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">MN</span> | Mine(s) | A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages. |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">MLM</span> | Ore Treatment Plant | A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration. |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;">PRT</span> | Port | A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor. |

I chose the three categories because each one shows a different stage around production. Mines show where materials are taken out of the ground, ore treatment plants show one stage of processing, and ports show where materials and goods can enter transport and trade networks.

It is also interesting to note how strict the GeoNames categories are. only means a place where people actually take mineral ore out of the ground, either from a pit or from underground. So a place can be connected to mining and still not count as MN.
MLM is even more specific. It does not mean every place where minerals are processed. It only means a plant where ore is treated to increase the amount of metal in it. So a refinery or smelter might be very important, but it would not necessarily show up under MLM.

> **My expectation:** I thought the map would be packed. China’s scale in mining, refining, manufacturing, shipbuilding, and cargo movement made me expect dense mine, processing, and port coverage.
>
> Also, the source file was **132.1 MB**, so waiting for the `.txt` file to load definitely did not help lower my expectations.

<span style="color:#FF5C8A;"><strong>Spoiler:</strong></span> they were not. The GeoNames results looked very different from the scale suggested by the wider statistics.

I initially planned to use a bounding box and focus on a smaller region, but after seeing the results, I decided to keep the full country view. The filtered data gave me only 41 mine records, 2 ore treatment plants, and 131 ports — a total of just 174 points.

There could be a few reasons for this. As discussed earlier, the GeoNames definitions are quite narrow, some sites may fall under different categories, and others may not be publicly recorded or included in the database. In some cases, detailed information about certain industrial or extraction sites may also be limited because making their exact locations public could create security concerns. GeoNames may also simply have stronger coverage for some types of places than others.

Figure 2 helps explain why many of the port records appear where they do. Around Shanghai (上海), the <span style="color:#FF7A00;">Yangtze River (长江)</span> connects inland cities such as Wuhan (武汉) and Nanjing (南京) with the east coast. This river has long acted as one of China’s major transport corridors, so the concentration of ports along it reflects more than just coastline: it also shows how rivers connect inland production and trade with the sea.

Farther south, the <span style="color:#E6B800;">Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲)</span> brings together Guangzhou (广州), Shenzhen (深圳), and Hong Kong (香港). The region became especially important after China’s economic reforms began in the late 1970s. Shenzhen was developed as one of China’s first Special Economic Zones, while investment from Hong Kong helped expand manufacturing and export activity across the delta. The World Bank describes the region’s growth as closely connected to foreign investment, manufacturing, and major investments in ports and transport infrastructure. [Read more here.](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/578111468027858159/pdf/472420PUB0Box31UBLIC109780821376416.pdf)

This helps explain why the port records are so concentrated in these two areas. China’s [2025 National Economic and Social Development communiqué](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202602/t20260228_1962661.html) reported around **18.3 billion tonnes of freight and 354.47 million standard containers** moving through Chinese ports, showing the scale of the transport system behind the patterns visible on the map.

Figure 3 shows how different the two ore treatment plant locations are. The first is near Nanjing (南京), directly on the Yangtze and within the wider industrial region linking Nanjing and Shanghai. Its proximity to Nanjing Iron & Steel Union Co., Ltd. places it in an area where heavy industry, river transport, and access to coastal ports overlap.

The second record is on Hainan Island (海南岛), near Changhua Qianxinchang on the western coast. This location initially looks more isolated, but Hainan has a much longer mining history than the map suggests. A UN regional study notes that heavy-mineral beach sands on Hainan began to be mined in the late 1950s, including minerals such as ilmenite, zircon, monazite, and rutile. [The report can be found here.](https://repository.unescap.org/bitstream/20.500.12870/6098/1/ESCAP-1985-RP-Mineral-sands-Asia-Pacific.pdf)

So even though the two ore treatment plant records are too few to form a clear national pattern, their locations are still meaningful. One sits within a major eastern industrial and transport corridor, while the other sits in a region with a documented history of mineral extraction. This suggests that looking only at the number of points can hide important historical and economic context behind where those points are located.





















China’s 2024 natural resources reporting recorded 30,391 mining rights across the country. A mining right is a legal right or permit connected to mineral extraction, not a count of physical mines, so the two figures cannot be compared one-to-one. Still, the scale makes it clear that the 41 GeoNames mine records represent only a small part of mining activity in China.

Spatially, the <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">mine records</span> spread much further inland, while the <span style="color:#4361EE;">port records</span> cluster around eastern and southern China.

Kitchin and Lauriault argue that data is never completely neutral or “raw.” Data is produced through choices about what to measure, how to classify it, which standards to use, and which systems record it. They describe data as “situated, contingent, relational, and framed,” meaning that the dataset already reflects decisions made before anyone begins analyzing it. The same applies here: GeoNames shapes what becomes visible through its categories and records, while my own choices about feature codes, colors, highlights, and basemaps shape how those records are interpreted.

I also drew a heat-map-style layer over the map to look more closely at whether the mine records appeared to line up with areas of higher terrain, especially since many of the mine points sit further inland. Visually, this made the mines seem much more connected to mountainous areas. But that is also where the map can become misleading. By adding the heat-map layer myself, I am making that relationship look stronger and potentially creating a causal connection between terrain and mining that the GeoNames data does not actually prove.


## GeoNames as a Data Assemblage

I believe this connects closely to Kitchin and Lauriault’s idea of a data assemblage: data is not just neutral information that exists on its own. It is shaped by the people, institutions, technologies, rules, and choices involved in creating it. In GeoNames, the points on my map were affected by who collected the data, how places were classified, who edited the records, and how the information was stored. GeoNames also has a China ambassador, and it lists sources such as the National Bureau of Statistics of China and Beijing City Lab, showing that the dataset is built from different people and institutions rather than one single source. Then I added my own choices by deciding which feature codes to use, filtering the data in R, choosing the colors, and designing the popups. So the final map is not simply a direct picture of mining and infrastructure in China. It is the result of many different decisions made along the way.

## A Note on the Coding and Formatting

I originally considered using a tighter bounding box so the selected records would fill more of the screen. Once I saw how few mine and ore treatment plant records existed, keeping China at the national scale made more sense.

Most of the changes I made to the original notebook came from trying to think about what would make sense to someone clicking on the map for the first time.

The original feature codes were not very intuitive. PRT is somewhat easy to guess, but MN and especially MLM are much less clear. I changed the popup so the reader sees the full feature type instead of only the code.

I also changed the colors so each category has a clear visual logic. <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">Brown represents mines</span> because the color connects to land and extraction. <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">Teal represents ore treatment plants</span> because the color feels more industrial. <span style="color:#4361EE;">Blue represents ports</span> because blue connects naturally to water and movement. The feature type inside each popup uses the same color as the point, so the popup and the map feel connected.

I also experimented with adding province names through a separate lookup table. The lookup introduced incorrect labels and the province information did not add enough to justify another manually created layer of data, so I removed the lookup.

The final popup therefore keeps only the information I actually use: name, feature type, latitude, longitude, and last updated date.

## Where I Could Use This Again
This project connects directly to my capstone on international law and critical minerals. I was hoping it would also give me more direction on how I could study critical mineral supply chains. In a bigger project, I could map mines, processing plants, ports, trade routes, companies, and different laws or regulations to better understand how critical minerals move from extraction to export.

I also see maps like this as incredibly useful from a policymaking perspective. They can make large amounts of information easier to understand and can help show where activity is concentrated, where important infrastructure is located, and where there may be gaps or risks. At the same time, this project reminded me that policymakers still need to be careful with what a map seems to show, because visual patterns can make relationships look stronger than the evidence actually supports.

One of the biggest lessons from this assignment was that proximity does not prove causality. A mine, processing plant, and port might appear close together on a map, but that does not mean one caused the other to be located there or that they are even part of the same supply chain. Proving that would require other evidence, such as trade data, ownership records, transport routes, or company information.

By the end of the project, I realized that maps can be incredibly useful for understanding critical mineral supply chains, but they also need to be questioned. They can show patterns and possible connections, but they cannot explain the full story on their own.
