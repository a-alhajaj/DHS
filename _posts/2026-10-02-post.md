China makes and processes a huge amount of what the world uses, but I wanted to look at the physical side of that. **Where are the mines? Where does processing happen? And where are the ports that move these materials across China and beyond?**

Part of why I chose China is personal. I just came back from studying away in Shanghai, and I miss the country. China is also a really interesting place to map because mining, processing, manufacturing, transport, ports, cities, and trade all overlap in different ways.

China is also a major producer and processor of critical minerals. That made me think a map of mines, processing facilities, and ports would probably show a dense network.

The GeoNames dataset did not match that expectation.

## What I Decided to Map

I did not want to map everything, so I chose three GeoNames features that felt like they could tell part of the same story: <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>mines</strong></span>, <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>ore treatment plants</strong></span>, and <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>ports</strong></span>.

| **Feature Code** | **Feature** | **GeoNames definition** |
|:---:|---|---|
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>MN</strong></span> | **Mine(s)** | A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages. |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>MLM</strong></span> | **Ore Treatment Plant** | A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration. |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>PRT</strong></span> | **Port** | A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor. |

[Explore the full GeoNames feature code list](https://www.geonames.org/export/codes.html)

The logic was simple. Mines show **extraction**, ore treatment plants show one part of **processing**, and ports show **movement**.

I knew from the beginning that those three categories would not create a full supply chain. The GeoNames file does not include factory links, company relationships, railway movements, commodity flows, or shipping routes. But the three categories were enough to start asking a spatial question: what does China’s extractive and transport landscape look like when these records are placed together?

Before looking at the data, I expected mines across major resource regions, more processing records around industrial areas, and a dense concentration of ports around eastern and southern China.

Then I filtered the dataset.

## Okay... where is everything?

After filtering the China GeoNames file, I was left with only **173 records**: around **40 mines, 2 ore treatment plants, and 131 ports**.

| **Feature** | **Records** |
|---|---:|
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>Mine(s)</strong></span> | **~40** |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>Ore Treatment Plant</strong></span> | **2** |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>Port</strong></span> | **131** |

The port count did not surprise me much. The mine and ore treatment plant counts did.

Around 40 mine records for all of China already felt low. But **two ore treatment plants?**

That number forced me to stop reading the map too literally. GeoNames is not telling me that China has two ore treatment plants. GeoNames is telling me that the China file contains two records under the **MLM feature code**.

That difference matters.

China has a huge mining and mineral-processing sector, so the thin mine layer and almost empty processing layer say more about **GeoNames coverage and classification** than about China’s physical landscape.

Kitchin and Lauriault’s argument became much clearer at that point. Data does not simply sit in the world waiting to be collected. Data is produced through people, institutions, categories, standards, technologies, and decisions.

A mine can physically exist without appearing in GeoNames. Someone has to identify the mine, assign a name, record coordinates, classify the location, and add the record to the database.

### <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">No point does not mean no place.</span>

A missing point could mean no record was added, a different feature code was used, an older name was used, or GeoNames coverage is incomplete.

## What the Map Actually Shows

The map still shows some clear spatial patterns.

The <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>mine records</strong></span> stretch much further inland, while the <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>port records</strong></span> gather heavily around eastern and southern China.

At first, that pattern looked almost perfect for the story I wanted to tell.

My brain immediately went:

### <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">mine → processing → port</span>

But the GeoNames data does not prove that sequence.

The map contains no railway movements, company relationships, commodity flows, shipping records, or trade destinations connecting one marker to another. A mine located near a port does not prove that material from the mine moves through that port.

That is where *Do Maps Lie?* became useful for me.

A map does not need false information to create a misleading impression. A viewer can look at real points and build a relationship that the underlying data never established.

The spatial pattern may be real.

The explanation may not be.

## The points also flatten scale

Another thing I noticed is that the map makes very different places look almost equal.

A small local port and a huge container port appear as similar markers because both locations share the same GeoNames feature code.

Shanghai Yangshan Port is a good example. Yangshan appears as one blue marker, just like much smaller ports. The marker does not show cargo volume, container throughput, economic importance, or scale.

The marker tells me:

**this location is classified as a port**

The marker does not tell me:

**how important the port is**

The same problem appears with mine records. A small mining site and a major mining complex can both appear as one brown point.

The category may be correct, but the visual still removes differences that matter.

## The gaps became part of the map

I originally considered using a tighter bounding box so the selected records would fill more of the screen.

Once I saw how few mine and ore treatment plant records existed, I changed my mind.

A tighter frame would make the dataset look denser than the dataset really is.

Keeping the whole country visible makes the blank spaces easier to notice. In this project, the blank spaces became part of the analysis.

That choice also connects to *Do Maps Lie?* because map framing changes what the viewer notices.

Zoom in and the records look dense.

Zoom out and the missing areas become much more obvious.

The national view therefore shows both where GeoNames has records and where GeoNames has very little to show.

## A few examples made the problem even clearer

One example was **Wulong Jinkuang** in Liaoning. The GeoNames record gives very precise coordinates. When I compared the record with outside information about the current Wulong operation, the locations did not line up as closely as I expected.

That comparison made one point especially clear:

### <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">Precision is not the same thing as accuracy.</span>

Five decimal places can make a location feel extremely trustworthy, but decimal precision does not automatically mean the coordinate is correct.

Wafangdian gave me almost the opposite problem. Wafangdian is an important diamond-producing area, but the area does not appear clearly in my mine layer.

So one example contains a visible record that still needs checking.

The other example contains an important mining landscape that barely appears.

Those examples pushed me away from asking only, **“Is the map correct?”**

A better question became:

**What version of China does GeoNames make visible?**

## What is missing changes the story

The ore treatment plant layer is probably the clearest example.

GeoNames gives me only two MLM records.

If someone knew nothing about China and only looked at that layer, the viewer could easily assume that mineral processing barely exists.

That conclusion would be completely misleading.

The problem is not necessarily that the two records are false. The problem is that **two true points can still create a false sense of the wider landscape when so many relevant places are missing**.

GeoNames works well for questions like:

**Where is this named place?**

GeoNames is much weaker for questions like:

**What does the mine produce?**

**Who owns the mine?**

**How much does the mine produce?**

**Where does the material go?**

**How many people work there?**

**What environmental impacts are connected to the site?**

That limitation connects directly to Kitchin and Lauriault’s point that databases shape what questions can be asked.

The structure of the dataset limits the structure of the analysis.

## GeoNames is not just “the data”

The idea of a **data assemblage** started to make much more sense through this project.

GeoNames is not one person sitting down and mapping China.

GeoNames combines information from different institutions, datasets, contributors, technologies, classifications, and standards. GeoNames also allows users to edit and add records.

China has a GeoNames ambassador, and GeoNames lists the **National Bureau of Statistics of China** and **Beijing City Lab** among the national data providers.

But knowing the source list does not tell me where every individual point came from.

That provenance matters.

Some records may come from national institutions. Other records may come from international sources or individual contributors. Some records may have been edited later.

By the time I downloaded the China file, the records had already passed through multiple layers of decisions.

Then I added another layer.

I chose three feature codes.

I filtered those codes in R.

I assigned colors.

I decided what information appears in each popup.

I decided to keep China at the national scale.

So the final map is not simply **China according to GeoNames**.

The final map is also **my selection and presentation of GeoNames**.

That realization was one of the most useful parts of the assignment because the mapmaker also becomes part of the data story.

## A quick note on the coding and formatting

Most of the changes I made to the original code came from trying to put myself in the shoes of someone clicking on the map for the first time.

The feature codes `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT` are not especially intuitive. `PRT` is somewhat guessable, but `MLM` definitely is not. I therefore changed the popup to show the full feature name instead of only the code.

I also wanted the colors to feel intentional.

I used <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>brown for mines</strong></span> because brown connects visually to land and extraction, <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>teal for ore treatment plants</strong></span> because teal felt more industrial, and <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>blue for ports</strong></span> because blue connects naturally to water and movement.

The popup feature type uses the same color as the map point so the visual language stays consistent.

I originally included elevation in the popup, but many elevation values were missing and elevation was not helping the analysis.

Once I started checking individual locations, **latitude and longitude** became much more useful, so I replaced elevation with coordinates.

I also experimented with adding province names through a separate lookup table.

That choice created unnecessary problems.

Some administrative codes were matched incorrectly, and the province names were not adding enough value to justify another manually created layer of information.

I removed the province lookup and kept the popup simple:

**name, feature type, latitude, longitude, and last updated**

That final version feels much clearer.

## Where I could actually use this again

The workflow connects very directly to my capstone on **international law and critical minerals**.

For a larger project, I could combine mine locations with processing facilities, ports, ownership structures, trade routes, export restrictions, and legal or regulatory frameworks.

That kind of map could help me ask much bigger questions.

Where are critical minerals extracted?

Where are critical minerals processed?

Which countries control different stages of the supply chain?

Which ports matter most?

Where do trade law, environmental regulation, investment law, or export controls become important?

A map could be really useful for showing those relationships.

But this assignment also gave me one warning I would carry directly into the capstone:

### **A map can show where something is. A map cannot automatically prove how two places are connected.**

That matters a lot for critical minerals.

A mine, a processing facility, and a port might sit close together on a map.

The three locations may belong to the same supply chain.

The three locations may also have no direct relationship.

Proving the connection would require trade data, ownership data, transport records, company information, or other evidence.

So I started the assignment asking:

### **“Made in China... but where in China?”**

By the end, the better questions became:

### **Recorded by who? Classified how? And what is missing?**
