I am sure you have heard the phrase <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China.”</span> You have probably also seen headlines about Chinese cars, electronics, luxury brands, or China’s role in rare earths, but although we constantly hear about how much China produces, processes, and exports, I wanted to look at the physical geography behind all of that and ask a much simpler question: **where is this activity actually happening?** Part of why I chose China is because I just came back from studying away in Shanghai and I miss it, but it is also an insanely interesting country to map because extraction, processing, manufacturing, transport, and trade all overlap across an enormous and varied landscape.

China’s scale is difficult to overstate, because according to the [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china), in 2024 it produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**, while the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary) reports that China also leads the refining of **19 of the 20 strategic minerals** it tracks, with an average global market share of around **70%**. The same scale appears in shipping, because according to China’s [Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://www.miit.gov.cn/gxsj/tjfx/zbgy/mycb/art/2026/art_fc5e4ed1d3f5421885e6c3afff91cbbc.html), China accounted for **56.1% of global shipbuilding output in 2025**, while the [State Council of China](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202606/30/content_WS6a43bc0fc6d00ca5f9a0bf02.html) reported that Chinese ports handled around **18.3 billion tonnes of cargo and 354 million TEUs**, so it was one thing to keep reading numbers like these and another thing to actually open GeoNames and try to see what this enormous system looked like on a map.

## Interactive Map: China in Points

<iframe src="/DHS/assets/maps/CN_featuremap.final.html" width="100%" height="750" style="border:none;"></iframe>

Rather than trying to map everything connected to Chinese production, which would have become completely unmanageable, I chose three [GeoNames feature codes](https://www.geonames.org/export/codes.html) that seemed to capture different stages of the system: <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">`MN` for mines</span>, which GeoNames defines as places where mineral ores are extracted from the ground; <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">`MLM` for ore treatment plants</span>, meaning facilities where the metal content of ore is improved through concentration; and <span style="color:#4361EE;">`PRT` for ports</span>, meaning places with facilities for loading and unloading waterborne cargo or passengers. Together, these seemed like a useful way of looking at extraction, one form of processing, and movement into larger transport networks, although the definitions quickly became important because `MN` does not include every place connected to mining, while `MLM` is even narrower and does not include every refinery, smelter, quarry, factory, or storage facility that might matter to China’s mineral industry.

Going into the assignment, I genuinely expected the map to be packed, because a country doing this much extraction, refining, manufacturing, shipbuilding, and cargo movement should require an enormous amount of infrastructure, and the fact that my original GeoNames `.txt` file was **132.1 MB** and took forever to load definitely did not lower those expectations; however, after filtering the data, I ended up with only **41 mines, 2 ore treatment plants, and 131 ports**, giving me just **174 points across all of China**, which immediately became much more interesting than the dense map I thought I was going to get.

I originally planned to use a bounding box and zoom into a smaller region so the points would fill more of the screen, but once I saw how few mine and ore treatment plant records existed, I decided to keep the full-country view because the empty space had become part of the story rather than something I wanted to crop out, especially since the three layers looked completely different once they were placed together.

## What the Map Shows

The clearest pattern comes from the ports, because the <span style="color:#4361EE;">131 port records</span> dominate the map while the <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">41 mines</span> are spread further inland and the <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">two ore treatment plants</span> are almost easy to miss, which means that when all three layers are visible the map begins to look like a map of Chinese ports even though that does not mean ports are somehow the most important part of China’s production system; instead, it simply reflects the fact that GeoNames contains far more `PRT` records than `MN` or `MLM` records under the codes I selected.

Around <span style="color:#E63946;">Shanghai (上海)</span>, many port records sit near the <span style="color:#FF7A00;">Yangtze River (长江)</span>, which links inland cities such as <span style="color:#E63946;">Wuhan (武汉)</span> and <span style="color:#E63946;">Nanjing (南京)</span> with the east coast, while farther south another major concentration appears around the <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲)</span>, including <span style="color:#E63946;">Guangzhou (广州)</span>, <span style="color:#E63946;">Shenzhen (深圳)</span>, and <span style="color:#E63946;">Hong Kong (香港)</span>. The Pearl River Delta became especially important after China’s economic reforms began in the late 1970s, when Shenzhen became one of China’s first Special Economic Zones and investment from Hong Kong contributed to expanding manufacturing and exports, while a [World Bank report](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/578111468027858159/pdf/472420PUB0Box31UBLIC109780821376416.pdf) connects the region’s growth to foreign investment, manufacturing, ports, and transport infrastructure.

China’s [2025 National Economic and Social Development communiqué](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202602/t20260228_1962661.html) reported around **18.3 billion tonnes of freight and 354.47 million standard containers** moving through Chinese ports, which helps explain why these coastal and river areas are so important, but drawing the Yangtze and Pearl River Delta onto my figure also showed me how easily a map can create a relationship that the underlying dataset does not actually prove, because the moment I added those lines the ports began to look like parts of one connected transport system even though GeoNames tells me nothing about cargo routes, trade volume, movement direction, ownership, or whether those ports actually belong to the same supply chain.

## The Ore Treatment Plants and Mines

![Locations of the two ore treatment plant records]({{ '/assets/images/figure5.png' | relative_url }})

<p align="center"><em><span style="color:#666666;">Figure 3. Locations of the two ore treatment plant records, with nearby places marked in red.</span></em></p>

The ore treatment plant layer was probably the biggest surprise because there are only **two records**, with one located near **Nanjing (南京)** on the Yangtze inside a major industrial area and close to Nanjing Iron & Steel Union Co., Ltd., while the second appears on **Hainan Island (海南岛)** near Changhua Qianxinchang, a region with a long history of mineral extraction, including heavy-mineral beach sands such as ilmenite, zircon, monazite, and rutile, according to this [UN ESCAP report](https://repository.unescap.org/bitstream/20.500.12870/6098/1/ESCAP-1985-RP-Mineral-sands-Asia-Pacific.pdf). Although these surrounding locations give the points useful context, proximity still does not prove that the Nanjing plant is connected to the steel company or nearby ports, just as Hainan’s mining history does not prove what the GeoNames point processed, because proving those relationships would require company records, ownership data, transport routes, mineral information, or trade data that GeoNames does not provide.

![Mine records with a hand-drawn terrain heat map]({{ '/assets/images/figure3.png' | relative_url }})

<p align="center"><em><span style="color:#666666;">Figure 4. Mine records with a hand-drawn terrain layer, with red and orange showing the highest terrain.</span></em></p>


I also wanted to test whether the mine records followed China’s highest terrain, because I initially assumed that many mines would appear across the high western and southwestern regions, but after drawing a basic terrain layer I found that many of the mine points instead appeared in lower or medium-elevation areas, which meant that my first assumption was simply wrong and reminded me that even when a visual pattern looks convincing, properly testing a relationship between mining and terrain would require actual elevation, geology, and mineral-deposit data rather than a hand-drawn comparison.

## The Missing Dots

The blank areas became even more interesting once I compared GeoNames with other sources, because **41 mine records and 2 ore treatment plants** feel extremely small beside the scale of China’s mineral industry, and a [2026 USGS geospatial study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr20261018/full) contains **932 mines and quarries and 1,506 mineral processing plants** across China. The categories are different, so I cannot directly compare the numbers, but the USGS data identifies major mineral-industry activity in **Yunnan, Henan, Hunan, Jiangxi, Shandong, Sichuan, and Inner Mongolia**, including major rare-earth and tungsten sites that barely appear in my GeoNames layers, while China’s [2024 Natural Resources Bulletin](https://www.cgs.gov.cn/zcwj/202503/t20250314_820792.html) reported **30,391 registered mining rights**, which are not the same as individual mines but still make it very clear that my 41 mine points capture only a tiny part of a massive system.

This completely changed how I read the empty areas on my map, because a blank area cannot simply be interpreted as **“there is no mining here”** when it might instead mean that a feature falls under another code, was never entered into GeoNames, has not been updated, or does not fit the definition I selected; even the dots themselves simplify reality, because a mine containing pits, tunnels, roads, buildings, processing areas, and storage facilities becomes one point, just as a port stretching across kilometres of coastline also becomes one point, meaning that a tiny mine and an enormous industrial site can look exactly the same.

## GeoNames as a Data Assemblage

This is where Kitchin and Lauriault’s idea of a data assemblage became much more useful to me, because in Towards Critical Data Studies they describe data as “situated, contingent, relational, and framed,” rather than something completely 'neutral' , because what eventually becomes data is shaped by people, institutions, technologies, standards, categories, and rules. That is exactly what I could see happening with GeoNames. Before I downloaded anything, someone had already decided what counts as a mine, which feature codes exist, which locations are included, what they are called, and how they are classified, while the [GeoNames China page](https://www.geonames.org/countries/CN/) shows that its data draws from sources including the **National Bureau of Statistics of China** and **Beijing City Lab**, alongside user contributions, meaning that I know something about how the dataset is assembled overall without necessarily knowing who created every individual point.

I then added another layer of decisions myself by choosing MN, MLM, and PRT, selecting the colours, point sizes, basemap, popups, figures, annotations, rivers, and terrain, and even removing province labels after discovering that my lookup table was not always accurate, which left the final popup showing only the name, feature type, latitude, longitude, and last updated date. The update dates were also interesting because only two points were updated in 2026, very few in 2025, and some in 2024, while many records are older, which does not make the data useless but does mean that, for a country changing as quickly as China, I cannot assume every point reflects what is happening on the ground today, especially since the date only shows when the GeoNames record was edited rather than when the physical site itself changed. In other words, GeoNames decides what can enter the dataset, while I decide what the reader eventually sees on my map.

A lot of those choices came from thinking about what would actually make sense to someone clicking on the map for the first time, because PRT is somewhat easy to guess, but MN and especially MLM are much less obvious, so I changed the popups to show the full feature name rather than forcing the reader to understand the codes. I also gave the colours some basic visual logic, using <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">brown for mines</span> because it connects to land and extraction, <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">teal for ore treatment plants</span> because it feels more industrial, and <span style="color:#4361EE;">blue for ports</span> because it connects naturally to water and movement, while keeping those same colours inside the popups so the points and their information feel connected. I also tested a separate lookup table for province names, but when it produced incorrect labels and did not add enough useful information to be justified, I removed it.

The Sheffield Methods Institute’s [*Do Maps Lie?*](https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/education/smi/q-step/why-numbers-matter) helped me make sense of this because my map never needed to contain false information to become misleading, since showing all three layers makes ports dominate, hiding ports makes mines suddenly appear more important, adding rivers makes ports seem connected, and adding terrain encourages the reader to think about elevation, even though the underlying points never changed at all.

## Where I Could Use This Again

This project connects directly to my capstone on international law and critical minerals because I originally thought that mapping mines, processing plants, and ports might help me see how materials move from extraction to export, but instead the map showed me exactly what information I would still need before I could make that argument, including companies, ownership, mineral type, production volume, roads, railways, shipping routes, trade flows, and the laws or regulations governing them.

Maps can make huge amounts of information much easier to understand, especially for research and policymaking, but **proximity does not prove causality or connection**, and two places can sit right beside each other without belonging to the same company, handling the same mineral, or participating in the same supply chain. I started this project asking, **“Where are China’s mines, processing plants, and ports?”**, but by the end I realized that the more accurate question is **“Where does GeoNames record places classified as `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT`, and what patterns appear when I map them?”**, which sounds like a small wording change but completely changes what I can actually claim from the data.
