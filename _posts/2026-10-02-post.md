I am sure you have heard the phrase <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China.”</span> You have probably also seen headlines about Chinese cars, electronics, luxury brands, or China’s role in rare earths. China makes and processes a huge amount of what the world uses, but I wanted to look at the physical geography behind that production.

Part of why I chose China is personal. I just came back from studying away in Shanghai, and I miss it. China is also a particularly interesting country to map because extraction, processing, manufacturing, transport, and trade all overlap across a huge and varied landscape.

China’s scale is hard to overstate. In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. These minerals are important for semiconductors, batteries, electric vehicles, renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

China also dominates refining, leading in **19 of the 20 strategic minerals** tracked by the IEA, with an average global market share of around **70%**. [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary)

The same scale appears in shipping. In 2025, China accounted for **56.1% of global shipbuilding output**, while Chinese ports handled around **18.3 billion tonnes of cargo** and **354 million TEUs**. [Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://www.miit.gov.cn/gxsj/tjfx/zbgy/mycb/art/2026/art_fc5e4ed1d3f5421885e6c3afff91cbbc.html) [State Council of China](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202606/30/content_WS6a43bc0fc6d00ca5f9a0bf02.html)

Without China, many global supply chains would look very different. Which brings me to my map: **where is this activity actually happening?**

## What I Decided to Map

I chose three [GeoNames feature codes](https://www.geonames.org/export/codes.html) that capture different parts of China’s production landscape:

| Feature Code | Feature | GeoNames definition |
| :---: | --- | --- |
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">MN</span> | Mine(s) | A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages. |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">MLM</span> | Ore Treatment Plant | A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration. |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;">PRT</span> | Port | A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor. |

I chose these three categories because each one represents a different stage around production. Mines show where materials are taken out of the ground, ore treatment plants show one stage of processing, and ports show where materials and goods can enter larger transport and trade networks.

The definitions also matter. `MN` does not mean every place connected to mining. It means a place where mineral ore is actually extracted. `MLM` is even more specific. It does not mean every place where minerals are processed. It only refers to a plant where ore is treated to increase its metal content. A refinery, smelter, quarry, factory, or storage site could therefore be important to China’s mineral industry and still not appear under the codes I selected.

This became important later because some of the blank spaces on my map may not represent missing places at all. They may represent places that fall under a different classification.

> **My expectation:** I thought the map would be packed. China’s scale in mining, refining, manufacturing, shipbuilding, and cargo movement made me expect dense mine, processing, and port coverage.
>
> Also, the source file was **132.1 MB**, so waiting for the `.txt` file to load definitely did not help lower my expectations.

<span style="color:#FF5C8A;"><strong>Spoiler:</strong></span> it was not packed.

## The Missing Dots

The blank parts of the map also matter. GeoNames gives me only **41 mine records and 2 ore treatment plants** for all of China. That is very small compared with the size of China’s mineral industry.

In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

A 2026 USGS geospatial study contains **932 mines and quarries and 1,506 mineral processing plants** in China. The categories are different from GeoNames, so the numbers cannot be compared directly. But the difference shows that the dataset you choose changes what appears on the map. [USGS China mineral-industry geospatial study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr20261018/full)

China’s [2024 Natural Resources Bulletin](https://www.cgs.gov.cn/zcwj/202503/t20250314_820792.html) also reported **30,391 registered mining rights**. A mining right is not the same as one physical mine, so this is not a direct comparison either. Still, it shows that the 41 GeoNames mine points are only a small part of a much larger system.

This means a blank area does not automatically mean there is no mining there. It may mean the place is under another feature code, was never added to GeoNames, has not been updated, or does not fit the category I selected.

So instead of asking **“Why is there nothing here?”**, I should ask **“Why does nothing appear here under the codes I selected?”**

That is a more accurate question.

The feature definitions are part of this problem. `MN` only covers places that fit GeoNames’ definition of a mine. `MLM` only covers a specific type of ore treatment plant. A refinery, smelter, quarry, factory, or storage site may be important but still not appear under those codes.

So some “missing dots” may not be missing places. They may just be classified differently.

There is also another problem: **a mine is not really a dot**. A mine can include pits, tunnels, roads, buildings, storage areas, and processing areas. A port can stretch across kilometres of coastline.

On my map, all of that becomes one point.

That point tells me where GeoNames records the feature. It does not tell me the size, output, ownership, mineral type, or whether the site is still active.

A small mine and a huge mine can therefore look exactly the same.

## What the Dates Tell Me

I kept the **last updated date** in the popup because it tells me something about the GeoNames record.

According to the [GeoNames data format](https://download.geonames.org/export/dump/readme.txt), this is the date when the GeoNames record was last modified.

It does **not** tell me when the mine or port opened. It also does not tell me when someone last visited the site or confirmed that it was still active.

This matters because my outside statistics are mostly from **2024 and 2025**, while the GeoNames records may have been updated at different times.

Downloading the file in 2026 does not mean every point was checked in 2026.

So the map mixes records from different dates and shows them together as if they all belong to the same moment.

## GeoNames as a Data Assemblage

This connects to Kitchin and Lauriault’s idea of a **data assemblage**. In [*Towards Critical Data Studies*](https://kitchin.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/towards-critical-data-studies-2018.pdf), they argue that data is shaped by people, institutions, technologies, standards, categories, and rules.

That applies directly to GeoNames. Before I even downloaded the file, someone had already decided what counts as a mine, which feature codes exist, which places are included, what they are called, and how they are recorded.

GeoNames also combines information from different sources. Its [China page](https://www.geonames.org/countries/CN/) lists sources such as the **National Bureau of Statistics of China** and **Beijing City Lab**, and GeoNames also allows users to add and correct information.

This means the China file does not come from one source. It is built from different people, institutions, and systems.

I also do not know which source created every individual mine, port, or treatment plant record. So I know something about where the dataset comes from overall, but not where every single point came from.

Then I made my own choices. I selected only `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT`. I chose the colours, point sizes, basemap, popups, annotations, rivers, and figures.

So there are two levels of choice. GeoNames decides what enters the dataset, and I decide what enters my map.

## Why *Do Maps Lie?* Matters

The Sheffield Methods Institute’s [*Do Maps Lie?*](https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/education/smi/q-step/why-numbers-matter) helped me understand that a map does not need to contain false information to be misleading.

The same data can look different depending on how it is shown.

That happens in my map too. If I show all three layers, the **ports dominate**. If I hide the ports, the mine pattern becomes clearer. If I add rivers, the ports look more connected. If I add terrain, the mines look more connected to elevation.

The points stay the same.

The way I read them changes.

That is the main lesson for me. A map can make one interpretation look obvious even when the data does not fully prove it.

My map can make proximity look like connection. It can make blank space look like absence. It can make one point look like a full industrial site.

That does not mean maps are useless. It means they need explanation.

## Where I Could Use This Again

This project connects directly to my capstone on international law and critical minerals.

At first, I thought mapping mines, processing plants, and ports would show me how materials move from extraction to export.

It did not.

Instead, it showed me what information is missing.

A real supply-chain map would need mines, processing plants, ports, companies, ownership, mineral type, production volume, roads, railways, shipping routes, trade flows, and the laws or regulations that govern them.

Only then could I show whether a mine, processing plant, and port are actually connected.

This also matters for policymaking. Maps are useful because they can show patterns quickly, but **proximity does not prove causality or connection**. Two places can be close without belonging to the same company, handling the same mineral, or being part of the same supply chain.

By the end of the assignment, I realized that my map is better at **raising questions** than proving relationships.

I started by asking:

**Where are China’s mines, processing plants, and ports?**

A more accurate question is:

**Where does GeoNames record places classified as `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT`, and what patterns appear when I map them?**

That is a small wording change, but it changes what I can actually claim.
