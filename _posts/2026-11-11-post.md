# Not Another AI Thing: Getting Lost in the Maps of *1 the Road*

When Professor Wrisley first brought up the idea of creating a map using AI, my immediate reaction was honestly, *not another AI thing*. I feel like AI has become one of those topics that everyone is suddenly obsessed with, a little like a song that goes viral and you end up hearing absolutely everywhere until you are sick of it. But then I actually looked at when Ross Goodwin created *1 the Road* in 2018, and I thought, wait, this is actually quite interesting. This was happening years before ChatGPT and the current AI craze, when using technology to generate a novel during a road trip was still a pretty unusual experiment. Goodwin was essentially trying to see what a machine could capture, process and turn into writing while travelling across the country. So, perhaps I was a little too quick to dismiss the assignment. What I found particularly interesting was that, even with all the technology involved, figuring out where the text was actually talking about was surprisingly difficult. A place name does not always point to one obvious location, and an AI-generated suggestion does not necessarily mean that the computer has understood the context. Sometimes, it meant spending an unreasonable amount of time trying to work out whether Sunoco was a petrol station or, as I initially thought, some sort of drink (which now in hinesight makes no sense).

The assignment was to explore how different methods could identify and locate the places mentioned in *1 the Road*, a novel generated during a road trip using a camera, microphone, GPS device and portable writing machine. Working with Khadijah during Section 6, we annotated references to places in the text and compared our annotations with locations identified through automated methods. I went into the exercise thinking we would mostly be identifying place names and plotting them on a map. Instead, it became a lot more about interpretation: deciding what counted as a place, figuring out which location a reference might point to, and questioning why different methods sometimes came up with completely different answers. It also made me think about how easily a map can make information look certain, even when the process behind putting those points there is anything but.

## Apparently, a Petrol Station Is a Research Problem

Our first task was annotation in [Recogito](https://recogito.pelagios.org/): highlight geographic references and see whether its gazetteer could identify them. I expected big cities to be manageable. The text, unfortunately, preferred gas stations, strangely spelled towns, intersections and other details that did not always cooperate.

On page 44, Goodwin's machine writes **"JFK Memorial Highway Toll Plaza: a toll plaza in Perryvilley."** Recogito returned *No automatic match found*. But a human can look at the toll-plaza clue, wonder whether "Perryvilley" is a mangled Perryville, and investigate instead of treating the missing result as the end of the road.

*Figure 1. Perryvilley produces no automatic match. A misspelling is a much bigger problem for a name lookup than for a reader who notices contextual clues.*

A similar problem appeared with **John Deere Landscapes**, described on page 76 as **"a garden center in Fayetteville Street."** No automatic match appeared there either. That does not prove the business never existed. It shows that small, specific commercial places can be harder to recover than a major city or state.

### When GeoNames Is Technically Right but Contextually Questionable

Then there was **San Jose**.

Recogito offered [GeoNames ID 5392171: San Jose, California](https://www.geonames.org/5392171/san-jose.html), complete with a very convincing pin. But the surrounding passage mentions the I-40/I-95 interchange and Benson in North Carolina (Goodwin, p. 75).

Is the machine referring to California, borrowing a name, or producing another strange combination of words?

The pin cannot answer that for us.

![San Jose gazetteer suggestion in Recogito]({{ '/assets/images/Screenshot%202026-11-11%20at%201.38.35%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 3. Recogito suggests San Jose, California, using GeoNames ID 5392171. The database entry is valid, but that does not automatically mean the place fits the sentence.*

This was one of the more interesting moments of the assignment because the problem was not necessarily that the technology failed to find a place. It found a perfectly legitimate place.

The question was whether that place actually made sense in the passage.

A geographic database can tell us where San Jose is. It cannot, by itself, tell us why San Jose has appeared in a sentence about Benson, North Carolina.

And considering the text was generated by AI, the strange combination might be part of the novel itself rather than simply an annotation mistake.

## The Sunoco Situation

Then there was Sunoco.

In the novel, Sunoco appears repeatedly as **"a gas station in Franklin Street"** (Goodwin, p. 109).

At first, I had no idea what Sunoco was. Once I established that it was a petrol station brand, I thought the problem was solved.

Unfortunately, there are multiple Sunoco petrol stations.

The same name appearing repeatedly does not necessarily mean different locations are being referenced. It might be the same station mentioned several times, or the text may be repeating information supplied to the writing system.

This made me think about the difference between recognizing a business and actually understanding its location.

An automatic system might recognize Sunoco as a named entity, but selecting the correct station requires additional context. This is where street names, surrounding locations, and the wider route become particularly important.

It also raises the problem of consistency between annotators. Two people might recognize the same business but annotate different portions of the text or choose different geographic coordinates.

Neither decision is necessarily unreasonable, but the results may appear as disagreements when mapped.

## When the Novel Starts Speaking in GPS Coordinates

Another interesting feature of *1 the Road* is that it sometimes gives geographic coordinates directly rather than describing a recognizable location.

On page 116, the text includes **"32.041069786 N, -86.506375815 W"**, followed by elevation and speed measurements.

This is not exactly what I normally expect from a novel.

Instead of describing the landscape through conventional language, the machine occasionally records geographic information much closer to what a GPS device would produce.

![GPS coordinates and speed in the novel]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-11%20at%201.44.49%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 4. Geographic coordinates, elevation, and speed appear directly in the novel's prose.*

This also reveals a limitation of named entity recognition.

A system designed to identify place names may successfully recognize "Baltimore" or "New Orleans" but require different rules to extract numerical coordinates.

There is also the complication of optical character recognition, or OCR, which was used to convert the printed novel into machine-readable text.

If OCR introduces strange spaces, misspellings, or missing punctuation, automated recognition becomes more difficult before geocoding even begins.

In other words, the technology might be excellent at identifying locations, provided the text has not already made a mess of them.

### Ashwood: An Unexpected Trip to Australia

One particularly dramatic example was Ashwood.

The manual annotation placed Ashwood at **36.0736, -86.4630**, while both automated methods selected **-37.8667, 145.1022**, in Australia.

According to the map, the difference was approximately **15,488.90 kilometers**.

That is quite a detour for a road trip through the United States.

![Ashwood geographic disagreement]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.22.55%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 6. Ashwood: the manual and automated predictions are 15,488.90 km apart.*

However, this does not automatically prove that the manual annotation is correct. The first thing to investigate is whether the same textual reference was matched across the datasets.

What the example demonstrates is how ambiguous place names can produce enormous geographic differences.

### Columbia: One Name, Different Continents

Another example was Columbia.

The manual annotation selected **34.0007, -81.0348**, corresponding to Columbia, South Carolina.

spaCy selected **4.0999, -72.9088**, placing the result in Colombia.

The map reported a difference of **3,429.44 km**.

![Columbia geographic disagreement]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.22.01%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 7. Columbia: a 3,429.44 km disagreement demonstrates the importance of geographic context.*

Here, the similarity between Columbia and Colombia becomes particularly interesting.

A human reader can consider the road-trip context and investigate the intended reference. An automatic system may select a plausible geographic result without sufficiently resolving the ambiguity.

### Belgium: When the Methods Almost Agree

Not every comparison produced a dramatic difference.

For Belgium, the manual and LLM coordinates were only **0.77 km apart**.

![Belgium geographic comparison]({{ '/assets/images/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.22.15%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 8. Belgium: Manual and LLM coordinates differ by only 0.77 km.*

This shows another type of disagreement.

Two methods may identify essentially the same geographic area but choose different reference points.

A small coordinate difference does not necessarily mean a meaningful disagreement about the place itself.

### Auburn Palace and Biloxie: When Agreement Is the Problem

Perhaps the most interesting results were not the disagreements at all.

They were the cases where the two automatic methods agreed perfectly.

For Auburn Palace, both spaCy and the LLM selected **-33.8743, 151.0207**, in Australia.

The distance between their predictions was **0.00 km**.

![Auburn Palace identical automated predictions]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.21.41%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 9. Auburn Palace: spaCy and LLM return identical coordinates in Australia.*

Then there was Biloxie.

Both automated methods selected **34.4154, -118.4502**, in California.

Again, the distance was **0.00 km**.

![Biloxie identical automated predictions]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.21.35%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 10. Biloxie: both automated methods agree on a California location, despite the novel's Biloxi, Mississippi context.*

This is a particularly useful example because the novel's journey includes Biloxi, Mississippi.

Two methods can agree perfectly and still produce a questionable geographic interpretation.

The matching coordinates may reflect similar geocoding assumptions or shared geographic lookup behavior.

This made me realize that **agreement between automated systems does not necessarily mean accuracy**.

It simply means that the systems produced matching results.

There is also a limitation in how Map 2 was constructed. The comparison code matches normalized place names and repeated occurrences, so some pairings remain candidate matches until they are checked against the original text.

The connecting lines should therefore be interpreted as an invitation to investigate, rather than definitive proof of error.

## What Gets Lost Between the Text and the Database?

Manual annotation allows the reader to question ambiguous names, investigate unusual spellings, and use the surrounding text to make decisions.

Automated geocoding works differently.

Named entity recognition attempts to identify geographic expressions. Geocoding then associates those expressions with locations in a database.

This process is efficient, but it depends on the information available and the assumptions built into the recognition and matching systems.

Places that are poorly represented in geographic databases, including smaller businesses, rural communities, and locations with alternative or historical names, may be harder to identify accurately.

Kenric McDowell makes an important point about Goodwin's Wordcar.

The system received information about nearby businesses through the Foursquare API, meaning that the geographic information available to it was already influenced by a commercial platform.

McDowell specifically mentions the prominence of gas stations and fast-food restaurants in the data (p. 28).

This brings me back to Sunoco.

Perhaps the presence of so many petrol stations was not simply a strange feature of the writing. It also reflected the types of locations available through the data that helped generate the text.

This means there were several layers of technological selection.

The Wordcar received geographic information, generated text from those inputs, and produced a manuscript. The published text was then interpreted through manual and automated annotation before being converted into maps.

By the time a place becomes a colored dot, it has already passed through several stages of interpretation.

The published book is also shorter than the approximately 200,000 words described as being generated during the original experiment. Mapping the published references cannot establish exactly how much material was removed or why. That would require comparison with the complete original output.

## So, Was It Another AI Thing?

Technically, yes.

But the assignment ended up being more interesting than I expected.

Map 1 helped me visualize the geographic information identified by the three methods.

Map 2 made it possible to compare the coordinates more closely and investigate where the methods agreed or disagreed.

What I found most interesting was not simply that automated systems sometimes made questionable choices.

It was how easily a precise geographic result could create the appearance of certainty.

Perryvilley showed how a misspelling could prevent a match. Sunoco demonstrated that recognizing a business name does not necessarily identify one specific location. San Jose revealed how a perfectly valid GeoNames result might still require contextual interpretation.

And Biloxie showed that two matching coordinates can be just as suspicious as two completely different ones.

I started the assignment thinking, *not another AI thing*.

I finished it realizing that perhaps the important question is not whether AI can locate a place, but whether it understands why that place belongs in the text.

And apparently, sometimes answering that question requires spending far too much time investigating a petrol station.
