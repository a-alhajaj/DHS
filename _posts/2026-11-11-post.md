I think we can all agree that AI has become a little impossible to escape. Every other conversation, article, or assignment seems to involve it somehow, so when Professor Wrisley introduced *1 the Road*, I was already preparing myself for *another AI thing*. But after finding out that it was from back in **2017**, long before ChatGPT became everyone's favourite topic, my intrest peaked. There is something quite fascinating about the idea of a car travelling from New York to New Orleans, collecting information about its surroundings and turning it into a novel, which was eventually published in **2018**. It was strange, experimental, and definitely not the kind of writing I was used to.

What made the experience even more entertaining was how unpredictable the geography became. I had expected a road trip equipped with GPS to be relatively straightforward to map, but the text had other plans. Some locations were harder to interpret than expected, and not every result could be confidently mapped. My favourite example was sunoco, which I spent far too long believing was some sort of juice brand. I had already imagined the packaging before discovering that it was, in fact, a petrol station.

Khadija and I annotated Section 6 together using Recogito in class. the remaining manual annotations were completed by everyone in our class across the text with the two automated datasets provided for the assignment. Afterward, I used the cleaned data to build my maps in Posit Cloud, comparing our manual annotations with the course-provided spaCy/geopy and LLM-assisted datasets.

## <span style="color:#11785e;">A Cadillac with Something to Say</span>

Goodwin's experiment involved a black Cadillac equipped with a camera, GPS, microphone and clock, connected to a machine that printed its writing onto what appeared to be a never-ending recipt. Convolutional neural networks interpreted images, while LSTM networks generated text character by character, producing something inspired by Jack Kerouac's On the Road.

<blockquote style="border-left:5px solid #7570b3; background:#f3f0fa; color:#332b50; padding:14px 18px; border-radius:0 10px 10px 0;">
<p style="margin:0;">“Typewriters are not cameras for writing. But artificial neural networks might be.”</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0; font-size:0.85em;">Ross Goodwin, Introduction, p. 12</p>
</blockquote>

The novel's first timestamp, **09:17:34**, appears alongside the rather mysterious statement that "the house was heavy" (p. 41). The timestamps suggest accurate documentation, while the sentences often sound more like very dramatic poetry. When the book was scanned and converted into text using OCR for our assignment, it also introduced another possibility for incorrect spelling or numbers.

## <span style="color:#b44900;">The Petrol Stations Were Already in the Data</span>

Something I initially overlooked was that Wordcar was not independently discovering every location it mentioned. Kenric McDowell explains that it borrowed nearby business information from the **Foursquare API**, which meant the machine's understanding of America was already shaped by a commercial location database. McDowell observes the abundance of "gas stations and fast-food restaurants" (p. 28), while acknowledging that rivers, bridges and parks also appeared. The businesses were not necessarily more important to human experience, they were particularly visible through the available data.

This relates to *volunteered geographic information* (VGI), where people contribute geographic knowledge through platforms such as OpenStreetMap. Foursquare and OpenStreetMap operate differently, but both help demonstrate how geographic information depends on who records it and what a platform prioritizes.

The novel mentions "a gas station in Franklin Street" three times between **23:26:21 and 23:27:09**, only 48 seconds apart (p. 109). But three mentions do not prove that the car passed three different stations. It might have been repeatedly generating text from one nearby business listing.

![Repeated Sunoco gas station references in Recogito]({{ '/assets/images/Screenshot%202026-11-11%20at%201.46.24%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})
*Figure 3. Recogito highlights three repeated references to Sunoco and a gas station on Franklin Street. These repetitions demonstrate how the machine could generate multiple mentions of a location without necessarily describing separate places.*


The same difficulties appeared during annotation. Recogito could not automatically identify **Perryvilley**, although the JFK Memorial Highway Toll Plaza mentioned alongside it suggested that Perryville, Maryland, was worth investigating (p. 44). In another passage, Recogito suggested San Jose, California, using GeoNames, even though the surrounding text mentioned an interstate interchange near Benson, North Carolina (p. 75). The database identified a real city, but that did not establish whether the city belonged in the sentence.

![Perryvilley annotation with no automatic match]({{ '/assets/images/Screenshot%202026-11-11%20at%201.37.01%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})
*Figure 1. Recogito cannot automatically identify Perryvilley.*

These examples helped me understand why human annotations can also differ. Different people might identify different levels of detail or accept different suggested locations. Our combined manual dataset even contains annotations at identical character offsets pointing to Tennessee and South Carolina. These are competing interpretations. It is not evidence of two diffrent journeys. 

## <span style="color:#6255a6;">How Many Points Should There Actually Be?</span>
One question I kept returning to was why our maps were not showing thousands of locations. According to McDowell, Wordcar's camera captured an image *every twenty seconds* (p. 26). This means three photographs per minute, or *180 photographs per hour*. The timestamps in the published narrative span approximately 75 hours, so if the camera operated continuously, it could theoretically have captured **75 × 180 = 13,500 photographs**. At a speed of twenty miles per hour, that would mean approximately 179 metres between images.

However, photographs are not the same as GPS readings, and neither automatically produces a named location. A photograph might generate a description of the landscape, while a Foursquare query could identify several nearby businesses. Even the coordinates printed in the novel did not necessarily appear at regular intervals. On page 116, for example, two GPS passages are recorded 98 seconds apart.

Looking at our actual datasets, <span style="color:#11785e;"><strong>manual annotation produced 245 mappable points</strong></span>, compared with <span style="color:#b44900;"><strong>248 from spaCy/geopy</strong></span> and <span style="color:#6255a6;"><strong>281 from the LLM</strong></span>. Although the automated methods identified more locations overall, many references were repeated or could not be matched to usable coordinates. What interested me was that having more points did not necessarily produce a clearer or more accurate map. The manual annotations generally followed the expected journey more closely, while the automated results were more geographically scattered. This made me question whether the number of locations identified was really the best way to measure how successful each method was.

The manual dataset contains 245 mappable records but only 131 distinct coordinate pairs, partly because the novel repeatedly mentions the same places. The LLM produces 33 more mappable records than spaCy but only 17 more distinct coordinate pairs. So although the automatic methods identify more references, that does not necessarily mean they identify more unique places correctly.

Something else I found interesting was that *1 the Road* sometimes gives us actual GPS coordinates instead of place names. For example, on page 116, the machine records **32.041069786 N, -86.506375815 W**, alongside its elevation and speed. Unlike a name such as Sunoco or San Jose, these numbers already identify a geographic position, so there is no need to guess which place they refer to. When I checked the two automated datasets, I found that both had **19 entries labelled `GEO`**, representing these numerical coordinate references. This is different from geocoding place names, where the computer first has to recognize a location in the text and then find its coordinates. For the spaCy/geopy method, spaCy helps identify the names, while geopy connects them to a geographic lookup service. I think this distinction is important because recognizing that something is a place does not necessarily mean finding the correct place on a map, especially when the novel contains ambiguous names.

I wanted to understand whether the points actually resembled the journey, so I overlaid possible Google Maps routes between New York and New Orleans onto screenshots of each layer. Nevertheless, the manual annotations produced the clearest concentration along the eastern United States, despite gaps through Virginia. The automated results were more geographically scattered, with points near Milwaukee, Toronto and other places that did not seem to fit the expected route.

When I zoomed out, the differences became even more noticeable. Both automated datasets placed locations across Europe, Asia and Australia, while the manual points were more concentrated around the United States. Of course, a distant reference is not automatically incorrect because the machine could mention somewhere without physically visiting it. Still, the worldwide view shows how ambiguous names and incomplete context can produce geographically questionable matches.

## <span style="color:#b44900;">I Wanted to Do Something More With the Map</span>

Once the original three-layer map was working, I wanted to take it a little further than the class exercise. The colored dots showed where each method placed its locations, but I couldn't tell whether their predictions were a few streets or an entire continent apart. So I decided to create a second interactive map where readers could click on a place and see those distances for themselves. I need to warn you that it is not perfect and since I'm not exactly the best coder in the world, I called in a favor from ChatGPT to help with the R/Leaflet code. It took a few attempts, including a version that zoomed dramatically without drawing any lines, but eventually it worked, allowing me to investigate the disagreements instead of just staring at scattered dots.

The second map connects candidate matches based on normalized names and occurrence order. Its distance calculations use the original coordinates. The lines make it easier to investigate individual disagreements rather than relying only on the overall patterns. However, matching locations by name and occurrence order does not guarantee that the methods are referring to the same passage, so the distances should be treated as comparisons between candidate matches rather than definitive measurements of error.

One particularly dramatic example is **Ashwood**, where the manual location and automated predictions are **15,488.90 kilometres apart**. The automatic methods selected a location near Melbourne, Australia, while the manual point was in Tennessee. That is quite a detour for an American road trip. However, the original passage says **“Manville, SC: a town in Ashwood”** (p. 86), meaning that the manual location also deserves investigation. Just because the manual point stayed in America does not automatically make it correct.

![Ashwood geographic comparison]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.22.55%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

Another interesting result was **Biloxie**, where both automatic methods selected identical coordinates in California, reporting a distance of **0.00 km**, despite the novel mentioning Hard Rock Hotel & Casino Biloxi in Mississippi (p. 120). Interestingly, this was not an isolated case. Across the two automatic datasets, I found <span style="color:#6255a6;"><strong>221 candidate matching records with identical coordinates</strong></span>. At first, that sounds like impressive agreement, but it does not necessarily mean the systems independently reached the correct answer. They may have relied on similar geographic lookups, and matching repeated place names across datasets is not always straightforward. The similarities between the methods could be just as revealing as their differences, especially when both agreed on a location that turned out to be incorrect.

## <span style="color:#6255a6;">What the Finished Map Leaves Out</span>

Matthew Wilson describes mapping as a *“cognitive process of **ordering** the world”* (2019, p. 50), which made me think about how much decision-making goes into something that eventually looks so straightforward. Foursquare influenced which businesses Wordcar knew about, while our annotations, coordinate choices and visualizations shaped what appeared on the final maps. Wilson's argument also extends beyond this assignment, since inaccurate geographic information could have much more serious consequences when mapping healthcare facilities or services in rural communities.

McDowell mentions that Wordcar generated approximately **200,000 words** during the journey (p. 18), yet the published narrative occupies only around **100 pages** (pp. 41–140). I wondered whether some of the gaps in our maps might reflect material removed during editing, but there is no way to establish that from the maps alone. Some passages may simply contain no identifiable locations, while others might have been missed or unsuccessfully geocoded. Without the complete original manuscript, we cannot determine how much was actually cut.

I started this assignment thinking *not another **AI** thing*, but ended up enjoying the process far more than I expected, especially when I decided to build something extra beyond the original map. The manual annotations generally produced a clearer picture of the journey, although they were not always correct, and the automated results demonstrated that even identical coordinates can be misleading. If anything, I learned that <span style="color:#11785e;"><strong>putting a pin on a map is relatively easy. Explaining why it should be there is the difficult part.</strong></span>
