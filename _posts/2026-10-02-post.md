And where are the ports that move these materials across China and beyond?**

## What I Decided to Map

I did not want to map everything, so I chose three features that felt like they could tell part of the same story: <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>mines</strong></span>, <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>ore treatment plants</strong></span>, and <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>ports</strong></span>.

| **Feature Code** | **Feature** | **GeoNames definition** |
|:---:|---|---|
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>MN</strong></span> | **Mine(s)** | A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages. |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>MLM</strong></span> | **Ore Treatment Plant** | A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration. |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>PRT</strong></span> | **Port** | A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor. |

[Explore the full GeoNames feature code list](https://www.geonames.org/export/codes.html)

Basically, I was thinking about **extraction, processing, and movement**.

Before seeing the data, I expected mines across major resource regions, more processing points around industrial areas, and lots of ports around the coast.

Then I filtered the dataset.

## Okay... where is everything?

After filtering the China GeoNames file, I was left with only **173 records**: around **40 mines, 2 ore treatment plants, and 131 ports**.

| **Feature** | **Records** |
|---|---:|
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>Mine(s)</strong></span> | **~40** |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>Ore Treatment Plant</strong></span> | **2** |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>Port</strong></span> | **131** |

The ports did not shock me that much. What really stood out were the other two layers.

Around 40 mine records for all of China already felt low. But **two ore treatment plants?**

That is where I had to stop myself from reading the map too literally. The map is not telling me that China has two ore treatment plants. It is telling me that GeoNames has two records under **this specific feature code**.

That difference is basically the whole point of this assignment.

Kitchin and Lauriault argue that data is not just sitting there waiting to be collected. It gets made through systems, classifications, people, and decisions. That became really obvious here. A mine can physically exist, but if it was never entered into GeoNames, or was classified differently, there is no point for me to map.

<span style="color:#FF5C8A;"><strong>No point does not mean no place.</strong></span>

## What the Map Actually Shows

There are still some really clear patterns.

The <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>mine points</strong></span> stretch much further inland, while the <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>ports</strong></span> collect heavily around eastern and southern China. At first, that looks almost perfect for the story I wanted to tell.

My brain immediately went:

### <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">mine → processing → port</span>

But... I cannot actually say that.

There is no flow data in this dataset. There are no railway routes, company links, commodity movements, or shipping records connecting one point to another. A mine being near a port does not mean material from that mine moves through that port.

This reminded me of *Do Maps Lie?*. The map does not necessarily have to contain false data to give you the wrong idea. Sometimes the problem is what **we assume** when we look at it.

The points also make very different places look equal. A small port and a huge container port can appear as basically the same dot. The map tells me that both are classified as ports. It does not tell me which one handles more cargo, which one matters more to trade, or what actually moves through either one.

## The gaps became part of the map

I originally thought about zooming in more and using a tighter bounding box.

But once I saw how few mine and processing records there were, I actually liked keeping the whole country visible.

If I cropped around the points, the dataset would look much fuller than it really is.

Keeping China at the national scale means you can actually see the blank spaces. And in this project, those blank spaces matter.

That connects again to *Do Maps Lie?*. Even something as simple as the frame of the map changes what the viewer notices. Zoom in and the points look dense. Zoom out and suddenly you see how much is missing.

So the map is not only showing where GeoNames has data.

It is also showing where it **does not**.

## GeoNames is not just “the data”

This is where the idea of a **data assemblage** started to make much more sense to me.

GeoNames is not one person sitting down and mapping China. It combines information from different institutions, datasets, contributors, classifications, and technologies. GeoNames also allows users to edit and add records.

China does have a GeoNames ambassador, and GeoNames lists the **National Bureau of Statistics of China** and **Beijing City Lab** as national data providers. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

But even knowing the sources does not mean I know where every individual point came from.

Then there is another layer: **me**.

GeoNames decided how places were classified. Then I chose three of those classifications. I filtered them in R. I picked the colors. I decided what information someone sees when they click. I decided not to crop the map.

So this is not just “China according to GeoNames.”

It is also **my version of GeoNames**.

## A quick note on the coding and formatting

Most of the changes I made to the original code came from trying to put myself in the shoes of someone actually using the map.

For example, `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT` are not exactly intuitive, so I changed the popup to show the full feature name. I also wanted the colors to mean something: <span style="color:#9A6B3F;"><strong>brown for mines</strong></span>, <span style="color:#2A9D8F;"><strong>teal for ore treatment plants</strong></span>, and <span style="color:#4361EE;"><strong>blue for ports</strong></span>. The same colors appear in the points and the popup so the map feels connected.

I originally had elevation in the popup, but there was a lot of missing data and I was not really using it. Once I started checking individual points, **latitude and longitude** were way more useful, so I swapped them in.

I also tried adding province names through a separate lookup table. That did not go very well. Some of the codes were matched incorrectly, and honestly, the region name was not adding enough to justify another layer of data, so I removed it.

Now the popup is much simpler: **name, feature type, latitude, longitude, and last updated**.

## Where I could actually use this again

This is probably the part of the assignment that connects most directly to my capstone.

My capstone is about **international law and critical minerals**, and this workflow gives me a really useful starting point for thinking spatially about that topic.

I could imagine mapping mines alongside processing facilities, ports, ownership, trade routes, or even different legal and regulatory frameworks. That could help me ask much bigger questions about where critical minerals come from, where they are processed, who controls different parts of the supply chain, and where international law actually enters the picture.

But I would also take one big warning from this assignment into that research:

**A map can show me where something is. It cannot automatically tell me how it is connected.**

And I think that ended up being the most interesting part of this project.

I started by asking, **“Made in China... but where in China?”**

I ended up also asking:

**“According to who, recorded how, and what is missing?”**
