I am sure you have heard the phrase <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China.”</span> You have probably also seen headlines about Chinese cars, electronics, luxury brands, or China’s role in rare earths. China makes and processes a huge amount of what the world uses, but I wanted to look at the physical side of that. **Where** are the mines? **Where** does processing happen? And **where** are the ports that move these materials across China and beyond?

China is also just a <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">really exciting place to map</span> because its landscape is connected to an enormous range of industries. In 2024, China produced 99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths. These materials are used across industries including <span style="color:#FF7A00;">electronics, renewable energy, cars, and advanced manufacturing</span>.

But China’s mineral landscape goes far beyond rare earths. It also has major deposits of coal, iron, gold, copper, and diamonds. Wafangdian in Liaoning, for example, contains more than 100 identified kimberlite bodies and more than half of China’s known diamond resources.

China also plays a major role in what happens <span style="color:#3D0C78;">after extraction</span>. According to the IEA, it is the dominant refiner for 19 of the 20 strategic minerals it tracks, with an average market share of around 70%.

From there, these materials feed into China’s huge manufacturing sector, producing everything from electronics and machinery to cars, jewellery, clothing, and consumer goods. This makes <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China”</span> much more than a label on a finished product. Behind it are large networks of raw materials, processing plants, factories, infrastructure, and transport.

And then comes <span style="color:#FF7A00;">movement</span>. In 2025, Chinese ports handled around 18.3 billion tonnes of cargo and 354 million TEUs. Ports therefore connect China’s internal industrial network to much wider <span style="color:#3D0C78;">global trade flows</span>.

It is one thing to hear about how much China makes and moves. It is another thing to actually try to map it.

##What I Decided to Map

I wanted to capture different parts of this landscape without trying to map everything at once, so I chose three GeoNames features: <span style="color:#FF7A00;">mines (MN) </span>, <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">ore treatment plants (MLN) </span>, and <span style="color:#3D0C78;">ports (PRT) </span>.


| **Feature** | **GeoNames definition** |
|---|---|
| **<span style="color:#FF7A00;">Mine(s)</span>** | “A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages.” |
| **<span style="color:#FF5C8A;">Ore Treatment Plant</span>** | “A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration.” |
| **<span style="color:#3D0C78;">Port</span>** | “A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor.” |

**Mines** show where extraction enters the landscape, **ore treatment plants** give some indication of what happens after extraction, and **ports** show where movement becomes visible. They do not create a complete supply chain, but they give enough of a structure to start asking spatial questions.
