When Professor Wrisley first brought up the idea of creating a map using AI, my immediate reaction was honestly, *not another AI thing*. AI has become that viral song you hear everywhere until you are sick of it. But then I looked at Ross Goodwin's *1 the Road*, published in 2018, and thought, wait, this is actually quite interesting. Goodwin used a car to collect information and turn an American road trip into machine-generated writing years before ChatGPT became part of everyday life. I soon discovered that finding out *where* the book was talking about was harder than expected. Sometimes that meant trying to decide which city a name referred to, and sometimes it meant discovering that Sunoco was a petrol station, not, as I initially suspected, a drink. In hindsight, that one was probably on me.

Working with Khadijah during Section 6, I used Recogito to annotate geographic references and then compared the human results with two automated datasets: spaCy with geopy, and an LLM-assisted approach. I expected to highlight places and watch dots appear, but the real work involved interpreting strange sentences and deciding whether a place had been visited or merely mentioned. 

## The Car Had a Database Before We Had a Map

Goodwin explains that Wordcar used a camera, microphone, GPS and clock to feed information into neural networks that produced language (Goodwin, pp. 12–13). But Kenric McDowell's account adds something crucial: the system also borrowed nearby location information from **Foursquare**, a platform cataloguing businesses and points of interest. McDowell notes the abundance of "gas stations and fast-food restaurants" (p. 28). The car saw America partly through the categories of a commercial database. A petrol station that Foursquare listed could become part of the story, while an unlisted local landmark might not. The selection started before our annotation work.

That explains some of the strange annotation work. Goodwin repeats **Sunoco**, described as a gas station on Franklin Street, three times in quick succession (pp. 108–109). Did the car encounter three stations, or did the generator keep returning to one business listing? Neither the name nor the repetition answers that. Meanwhile, Recogito could not automatically match the novel's **"Perryvilley"** beside the JFK Memorial Highway Toll Plaza (p. 44), although the toll-plaza clue suggests investigating Perryville, Maryland. With **San Jose**, Recogito proposed a real [GeoNames entry in California](https://www.geonames.org/5392171/san-jose.html), although the passage mentions an interchange near Benson, North Carolina (p. 75). Its precision did not resolve the context. The surrounding words mattered more than the confident-looking pin.

The text also occasionally prints latitude and longitude directly, alongside elevation and speed, rather than giving a recognizable place name. On page 116, for instance, the coordinates **32.041069786 N, -86.506375815 W** appear inside the prose. Named entity recognition (NER) detects names; numerical coordinates require other extraction rules. Even the spelling of the words is not guaranteed: the scanned book was converted using OCR, which can introduce errors before geocoding begins. After exporting my Recogito work, I had to check incomplete entries and move some coordinates from tags or comments into usable latitude and longitude fields. Cleaning also decided which places became visible.

## How Many Points Should There Be?

I kept wondering why the maps did not show a much denser trail. McDowell writes that Wordcar's camera took a picture **every twenty seconds** (p. 26). That works out to **3 photographs per minute**, or **180 per hour**. The published narrative runs from the morning of March 25 to midday on March 28, 2017, roughly **75 hours**. If the camera ran continuously throughout that interval, the calculation would be **75 × 180 ≈ 13,500 photographs**. At twenty miles per hour, twenty seconds of driving covers about **0.11 miles (180 metres)**. That sounds like a dense trail.

But there is a catch: a photograph is **not** a GPS reading, and neither is automatically a place name. A single Foursquare lookup could supply several nearby businesses, while many camera images might generate descriptions without identifiable places. The class dataset counts reported in the comparison examples give **243 mappable manual annotations out of 246 records**, compared with **248 out of 341 for spaCy/geopy** and **281 out of 374 for the LLM**. That means only **3 manual records** lacked usable coordinates, versus **93 in each automatic dataset**. Spread across roughly 75 hours, even 281 points would average only **3.7 per hour**, or one every **16 minutes**, although these are textual mentions, not regularly spaced observations. The gap reflects filtering and publication, not necessarily missing GPS data.

## What the Three Maps Actually Show

Using Leaflet in Posit Cloud, I plotted the methods as three switchable layers: <span style="color:#1b9e77;"><strong>green for manual</strong></span>, <span style="color:#d95f02;"><strong>orange for spaCy/geopy</strong></span> and <span style="color:#7570b3;"><strong>purple for the LLM</strong></span>. Jitter helps reveal markers that would otherwise sit on top of each other. Not being the world's greatest STEM coder, I called in a favor from ChatGPT for the R/Leaflet code. Some versions zoomed everywhere except where I wanted, which felt oddly appropriate.

I then placed screenshots of each layer alongside possible Google Maps routes between New York and New Orleans. Today's routes are only a reference, not proof of the exact 2017 drive. The **manual points follow the eastern corridor most clearly**, despite a noticeable gap through parts of Virginia and occasional outliers. The automatic layers contain slightly more plotted points overall, yet many are scattered away from the journey, including around the Great Lakes. In the LLM layer I could see markers near Milwaukee and Toronto. Unless the car had somehow developed teleportation, those are better understood as textual references or dubious location matches than actual stops on the trip. Their similar outliers may reflect shared lookup choices.

![Three methods against possible driving routes]({{ '/assets/1.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 5. My Google Maps route overlay makes the manual layer's route-shaped concentration easier to see.*

Zooming out to the whole world makes the problem harder to ignore. Both automated methods put dots across Europe, Asia and Australia, whereas the manual layer is comparatively concentrated in North America. Manual errors also exist, and distant mentions are not automatically wrong. Still, the worldwide view shows how easily a legitimate name match can become a misplaced pin when the system does not resolve the road-trip context. It also explains why counting dots alone is a poor way to judge success.

## When Agreement Is Just Two Machines Making the Same Choice

The other interactive map compares coordinates for candidate matching place mentions rather than displaying all the points at once. You can choose a location and see the distances between the available predictions. **[Explore Map 2 here](https://49d7f583f1204e698b032a09f2609c85.app.posit.cloud/file_show?path=%2Fcloud%2Fproject%2F02_click_to_compare.html&show=1).** For **Ashwood**, the manual point is in the United States while both automatic points are near Melbourne, Australia, a separation of **15,488.90 km**. The line looks ridiculous, but it raises a serious question: are the methods reading the same mention correctly, or has a shared name led them to different places? The map does not establish which one is right, and its matching of repeated names needs checking against the text.

![Ashwood comparison]({{ '/assets/Screenshot%202026-11-12%20at%2010.22.55%E2%80%AFAM.png' | relative_url }})

*Figure 7. Ashwood stretches the disagreement all the way to Australia.*

Even stranger, spaCy and the LLM agree to **0.00 km** on coordinates for **Biloxie** in California, although the novel's journey refers to **Biloxi, Mississippi**. Agreement does not make that interpretation convincing. Both workflows may have inherited a similar geographic match. A giant disagreement is obvious; an identical mistake hides behind a reassuring zero.

## The Pin Is Not the Whole Story

Matthew Wilson's **“Mapping”** helped me articulate what bothered me about treating these maps as answers. Mapping involves decisions, not just a finished image (pp. 50–55). Here the chain starts with Foursquare's commercial listings, continues through Wordcar's generated language and the published selection, and ends with annotators, geographic databases and my own design choices. McDowell says the machine produced around **200,000 words** (p. 18), but the maps cannot tell us what percentage the editors removed: an unmapped stretch could mean deleted text, no named places, an annotation omission or an unsuccessful lookup. That would require the complete original output for comparison.

So yes, the machines were faster, and the manual annotations were not flawless. But in this experiment the manual layer told a more coherent geographic story, while the automatic layers exposed the risks of taking a plausible match at face value. I started with *not another AI thing* and ended up thinking that maybe the interesting part was never whether a machine could put a point on a map. It was whether anyone, human or machine, had a good reason to put it **there**.
