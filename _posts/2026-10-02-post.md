I am sure you have probably heard the phrase <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China.”</span> You have probably also seen headlines about Chinese cars, electronics, luxury brands, or China’s role in rare earths. China makes and processes a huge amount of what the world uses, but I wanted to look at the physical geography behind that production.

Part of why I chose China is personal. I just came back from studying away in Shanghai, and I miss it. China is also a particularly interesting country to map because extraction, processing, manufacturing, transport, and trade all overlap across a huge and varied landscape.

China’s scale is hard to overstate. In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. These minerals are essential to semiconductors, batteries, electric vehicles, renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

China also dominates refining, leading in **19 of the 20 strategic minerals** tracked by the IEA, with an average global market share of around **70%**. [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary)

The same scale appears in shipping. In 2025, China accounted for **56.1% of global shipbuilding output**, while Chinese ports handled **18.3 billion tonnes of cargo** and **354 million TEUs**. [Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://www.miit.gov.cn/gxsj/tjfx/zbgy/mycb/art/2026/art_fc5e4ed1d3f5421885e6c3afff91cbbc.html) [State Council of China](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202606/30/content_WS6a43bc0fc6d00ca5f9a0bf02.html)

Without China, many global supply chains would look very different. Which brings me to my map: where is this activity actually happening?

## What I Decided to Map

I chose three GeoNames feature codes that capture different parts of China’s production landscape:

| Feature Code | Feature | GeoNames definition |
|:---:|---|---|
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">MN</span> | Mine(s) | A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages. |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">MLM</span> | Ore Treatment Plant | A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration. |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;">PRT</span> | Port | A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor. |

I chose the three categories because each one shows a different stage around production. Mines show where materials are taken out of the ground, ore treatment plants show one stage of processing, and ports show where materials and goods can enter transport and trade networks.

It is also interesting to note how strict the GeoNames categories are. only means a place where people actually take mineral ore out of the ground, either from a pit or from underground. So a place can be connected to mining and still not count as MN.
MLM is even more specific. It does not mean every place where minerals are processed. It only means a plant where ore is treated to increase the amount of metal in it. So a refinery or smelter might be very important, but it would not necessarily show up under MLM.

> **My expectation:** I thought the map would be packed. China’s scale in mining, refining, manufacturing, shipbuilding, and cargo movement made me expect dense mine, processing, and port coverage.
>
> Also, the source file was **132.1 MB**, so waiting for the `.txt` file to load definitely did not help lower my expectations.

<span style="color:#FF5C8A;"><strong>Spoiler:</strong></span> they were not. The GeoNames results looked very different from the scale suggested by the wider statistics.

## The Missing Dots

The next question is not just where the points are, but **what the blank spaces actually mean**.

GeoNames gives me only **41 mine records and 2 ore treatment plants** for all of China. That looks extremely small beside China’s role in mineral production. In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

A 2026 USGS geospatial study makes the difference even clearer. Its China mineral-industry database contains **932 mines and quarries and 1,506 mineral processing plants**. These numbers are not directly equal to my GeoNames counts because the USGS and GeoNames use different categories and definitions, but the difference still matters. It shows that the choice of dataset changes what becomes visible. [USGS China mineral-industry geospatial study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr20261018/full)

China’s own natural-resources data shows the same problem from another angle. The [2024 China Natural Resources Bulletin](https://www.cgs.gov.cn/zcwj/202503/t20250314_820792.html) reported **30,391 registered mining rights** at the end of 2024. A mining right is a legal right connected to mining, not one physical mine, so it cannot be compared directly with the 41 GeoNames points. Still, it shows that the GeoNames map is capturing only a small part of a much larger mining system.

This means I cannot look at a blank area on the map and simply say, “there is no mining here.” A blank area could mean there is no matching feature, but it could also mean that the place is listed under another category, was never added to GeoNames, has outdated information, or does not fit the exact feature code I selected.

This is where classification becomes very important. GeoNames defines `MN` as a mine where mineral ore is extracted from the ground. A place can still be connected to mining and not fit that exact definition.

`MLM` is even narrower. It does not mean every place where minerals are processed. It only refers to a plant where ore is treated to increase its metal content. A refinery, smelter, factory, quarry, storage site, or other important industrial facility could exist and still not appear under `MLM`.

So some of the “missing dots” may not actually be missing places. They may be places hidden by classification.

That changes the question from “Why is there nothing here?” to “Why does nothing appear here under the categories I selected?” That is a much more accurate way to read the map.

## What GeoNames Makes Visible

This connects directly to Kitchin and Lauriault’s idea of a **data assemblage**. They argue that data does not simply exist in the world waiting to be collected. It is produced through people, institutions, technologies, standards, categories, rules, and decisions.

That is exactly what happened before I even downloaded the China file. Someone had to decide what counts as a mine, what counts as an ore treatment plant, which feature codes should exist, which locations should be added, what those places should be called, how their coordinates should be stored, and when records should be updated.

GeoNames also combines information from different sources. For China, it lists sources such as the **National Bureau of Statistics of China** and **Beijing City Lab**, and it also has a China ambassador. This means there is no single person or institution creating “the China dataset.” It is built through different people, institutions, and systems.

However, knowing the sources used by GeoNames does not necessarily tell me which source created each individual point on my map. I know something about the provenance of GeoNames as a whole, but much less about the provenance of one specific mine, port, or treatment plant record.

That matters because different institutions collect different types of information, at different times, and for different reasons. One source may focus on population, another on administrative geography, and another on transport or local place names. When those sources are brought together, they do not automatically produce equal coverage across every type of feature.

This is why Kitchin and Lauriault describe data as **situated, contingent, relational, and framed**. The points are not simply “China.” They are China as recorded through this particular system.

## Then I Changed the Data Again

GeoNames made choices before I received the dataset, but I also made choices after downloading it.

I decided to select only `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT`. That single filtering decision removed almost everything else in the China file. I then chose the colours, point sizes, basemap, popup information, map scale, annotations, rivers, highlighted clusters, and which figures the reader would see.

So there are really two layers of selection. First, GeoNames decides what becomes data. Then I decide what part of that data becomes visible.

These choices did not change the original coordinates, but they changed what stood out. For example, using blue for ports makes them easy to connect visually with water. Making a point larger makes it easier to notice. Highlighting the Yangtze River makes nearby ports look more connected. Showing all 131 port records at once can make ports feel more important than mines simply because they dominate the screen.

This is why the final map cannot be treated as a neutral picture of China’s mining and transport system. It is a picture produced through several stages of selection.

## A Dot Is Also a Simplification

There is another limitation that is easy to miss: **a mine is not really a dot**.

A mine can include pits, underground tunnels, processing buildings, roads, storage areas, waste areas, and other infrastructure spread across a large area. A port can also stretch across kilometres of coastline and contain many separate terminals.

On my map, however, each one becomes a single coordinate.

That point tells me roughly where the feature is recorded, but it does not tell me how large it is, how much it produces, what mineral it handles, who owns it, whether it is currently active, how many people work there, or how important it is.

This also means that two dots can look equal even when the places they represent are completely different. A small local port and one of the world’s largest ports can both appear as one blue circle. A small mine and a major extraction site can also look the same.

So even if GeoNames recorded every location perfectly, the map would still simplify the real industrial landscape.

## Why *Do Maps Lie?* Matters Here

*Do Maps Lie?* helped me think about this beyond simple mistakes or incorrect coordinates. A map does not need to contain false information to be misleading.

The London house-price example in the video shows that the same underlying data can create different impressions depending on how values are grouped and displayed. My map does something similar through selection and design rather than price categories.

If I show all three layers, the **131 port points dominate**. If I hide the ports, the inland mine pattern becomes much easier to see. If I add rivers, the ports begin to look connected. If I add terrain, the mines begin to look related to elevation. If I make one type of marker larger, it becomes easier to notice even though its real-world importance has not changed.

The coordinates stay the same.

The story does not.

That is the part of *Do Maps Lie?* that matters most for this project. The problem is not always that a map contains something false. Sometimes the problem is that the map makes one interpretation feel obvious.

My map can make proximity look like connection, blank space look like absence, and a single point look like a complete representation of a large industrial site. That is why the map needs explanation alongside it.

## A Note on the Coding and Formatting

I originally considered using a tighter bounding box so the selected records would fill more of the screen. Once I saw how few mine and ore treatment plant records existed, keeping China at the national scale made more sense.

Most of the changes I made to the original notebook came from trying to think about what would make sense to someone clicking on the map for the first time.

The original feature codes were not very intuitive. PRT is somewhat easy to guess, but MN and especially MLM are much less clear. I changed the popup so the reader sees the full feature type instead of only the code.

I also changed the colors so each category has a clear visual logic. <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">Brown represents mines</span> because the color connects to land and extraction. <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">Teal represents ore treatment plants</span> because the color feels more industrial. <span style="color:#4361EE;">Blue represents ports</span> because blue connects naturally to water and movement. The feature type inside each popup uses the same color as the point, so the popup and the map feel connected.

I also experimented with adding province names through a separate lookup table. The lookup introduced incorrect labels and the province information did not add enough to justify another manually created layer of data, so I removed the lookup.

The final popup therefore keeps only the information I actually use: name, feature type, latitude, longitude, and last updated date.
