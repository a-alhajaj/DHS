I am sure you have heard the phrase <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">“Made in China.”</span> You have probably also seen headlines about Chinese cars, electronics, luxury brands, or China’s role in rare earths. China makes and processes a huge amount of what the world uses, but I wanted to look at the physical geography behind that production.

Part of why I chose China is personal. I just came back from studying away in Shanghai, and I miss it. China is also a particularly interesting country to map because extraction, processing, manufacturing, transport, and trade all overlap across a huge and varied landscape.

China’s scale is hard to overstate. In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. These minerals are important for semiconductors, batteries, electric vehicles, renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

China also dominates refining, leading in **19 of the 20 strategic minerals** tracked by the IEA, with an average global market share of around **70%**. [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary)

The same scale appears in shipping. In 2025, China accounted for **56.1% of global shipbuilding output**, while Chinese ports handled around **18.3 billion tonnes of cargo** and **354 million TEUs**. [Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://www.miit.gov.cn/gxsj/tjfx/zbgy/mycb/art/2026/art_fc5e4ed1d3f5421885e6c3afff91cbbc.html) [State Council of China](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202606/30/content_WS6a43bc0fc6d00ca5f9a0bf02.html)

Without China, many global supply chains would look very different. Which brings me to my map: **where is this activity actually happening?**

## What I Decided to Map

I chose three [GeoNames feature codes](https://www.geonames.org/export/codes.html) that capture different parts of China’s production landscape:

| Feature Code | Feature | GeoNames definition |
| :---: | --- | --- |
| <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">MN</span> | Mine(s) | A site where mineral ores are extracted from the ground by excavating surface pits and subterranean passages. |
| <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">MLM</span> | Ore Treatment Plant | A facility for improving the metal content of ore by concentration. |
| <span style="color:#4361EE;">PRT</span> | Port | A place provided with terminal and transfer facilities for loading and discharging waterborne cargo or passengers, usually located in a harbor. |

I chose these three categories because each one represents a different stage around production. Mines show where materials are taken out of the ground, ore treatment plants show one stage of processing, and ports show where materials and goods can enter larger transport and trade networks.

The definitions also matter. `MN` does not mean every place connected to mining. It means a place where mineral ore is actually extracted. `MLM` is even more specific. It does not mean every place where minerals are processed. It only refers to a plant where ore is treated to increase its metal content. A refinery, smelter, quarry, factory, or storage site could therefore be important to China’s mineral industry and still not appear under the codes I selected.

This became important later because some of the blank spaces on my map may not represent missing places at all. They may represent places that fall under a different classification.

> **My expectation:** I thought the map would be packed. China’s scale in mining, refining, manufacturing, shipbuilding, and cargo movement made me expect dense mine, processing, and port coverage.
>
> Also, the source file was **132.1 MB**, so waiting for the `.txt` file to load definitely did not help lower my expectations.

<span style="color:#FF5C8A;"><strong>Spoiler:</strong></span> it was not packed.

## What the Map Shows

The three feature types are distributed very differently. The <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">mine records</span> are spread much further inland, while the <span style="color:#4361EE;">port records</span> are concentrated much more heavily in eastern and southern China. The two <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">ore treatment plants</span> are so few that they almost disappear when all three layers are shown together.

This difference matters because the number of points changes what the viewer notices. There are **131 port records**, compared with only **41 mine records and 2 ore treatment plants**. When all the layers are turned on, the ports naturally become the strongest visual layer.

That does not mean ports are the most important part of China’s production system. It means GeoNames contains many more records under `PRT` than under the two mining-related codes I chose.

## Ports, Rivers, and the Story the Map Starts to Tell

The port layer has the clearest spatial pattern. Around <span style="color:#E63946;">Shanghai (上海)</span>, many records sit close to the <span style="color:#FF7A00;">Yangtze River (长江)</span>, which connects inland cities such as <span style="color:#E63946;">Wuhan (武汉)</span> and <span style="color:#E63946;">Nanjing (南京)</span> with the east coast.

Farther south, the <span style="color:#FF5C8A;">Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲)</span> contains another large concentration around <span style="color:#E63946;">Guangzhou (广州)</span>, <span style="color:#E63946;">Shenzhen (深圳)</span>, and <span style="color:#E63946;">Hong Kong (香港)</span>.

The Pearl River Delta became especially important after China’s economic reforms began in the late 1970s. Shenzhen was developed as one of China’s first Special Economic Zones, while investment from Hong Kong helped expand manufacturing and export activity across the delta. The World Bank describes the region’s growth as closely connected to foreign investment, manufacturing, and major investment in ports and transport infrastructure. [World Bank report](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/578111468027858159/pdf/472420PUB0Box31UBLIC109780821376416.pdf)

China’s [2025 National Economic and Social Development communiqué](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202602/t20260228_1962661.html) reported around **18.3 billion tonnes of freight and 354.47 million standard containers** moving through Chinese ports. This helps explain why major coastal and river regions matter so much to China’s transport system.

However, this is also where the map can start to tell a stronger story than the GeoNames data actually proves.

By drawing the Yangtze River and Pearl River Delta onto the figure, I make the nearby port records look connected, almost like one clear transport network. That connection may make sense based on wider knowledge of China’s transport system, but the GeoNames data itself does not tell me that cargo moves between these exact points. It does not show routes, direction of movement, cargo volume, company relationships, or whether the ports belong to the same supply chain.

Without the added lines, the map mainly says:

**there are port records here.**

After I add rivers and highlighted regions, it can start to look like:

**these ports are connected through one system.**

That second statement requires more evidence.

The annotations therefore do more than make the figure easier to read. They change the argument the map appears to make.

## Looking at Processing More Closely

The ore treatment plant layer is almost the opposite of the port layer. Instead of 131 records, GeoNames gives me only **two**.

The first record is near **Nanjing (南京)**, directly on the Yangtze and inside the wider industrial region linking Nanjing and Shanghai. It is also close to Nanjing Iron & Steel Union Co., Ltd. This places the point in an area where heavy industry, river transport, and access to coastal ports exist close together.

But geographic closeness is not proof of a supply-chain relationship. The map does not show that this ore treatment plant supplies the steel company or that nearby ports carry its material. Proving that would require other evidence such as ownership records, company information, transport routes, trade data, or supply contracts.

The second record is on **Hainan Island (海南岛)** near Changhua Qianxinchang on the western coast. At first, this location looks much more isolated. However, Hainan has a much longer mining history than the GeoNames layer suggests. A UN regional study notes that heavy-mineral beach sands on Hainan began to be mined in the late 1950s, including ilmenite, zircon, monazite, and rutile. [UN ESCAP report](https://repository.unescap.org/bitstream/20.500.12870/6098/1/ESCAP-1985-RP-Mineral-sands-Asia-Pacific.pdf)

The two ore treatment plant records therefore sit in very different contexts. One sits inside a major eastern industrial and transport region, while the other sits in an area with a documented history of mineral extraction.

Looking at those points in context tells me more than simply saying there are only two of them.

At the same time, what is missing is just as important. I expected mines, processing plants, and ports to form something that looked more like a clear supply chain. Instead, there is very little visible overlap between the three layers.

That does **not** mean the supply chain is disconnected.

It means GeoNames is not designed to show the relationships between those stages.

GeoNames gives me **places**.

A supply chain needs **connections between places**.

In Figure 4, the red and orange areas show the highest terrain, mainly in western and southwestern China. Surprisingly, most of the mine records do **not** fall inside those areas. Many sit in lower or medium-elevation regions instead, with only one clear outlier in the far west.

This was useful because my first assumption did not really hold up.

I saw that many of the mine points were inland and assumed that mountainous terrain might explain their location. Once I added the terrain layer, the relationship looked much weaker than I expected.

This is another reason visual patterns need to be checked rather than assumed. It is easy to place two things on the same map and immediately start building a story about why they are connected.

The terrain layer can help me ask:

**Are mining locations related to elevation or geology?**

It cannot answer that question.

To test it properly, I would need actual elevation, geological, and mineral-deposit data rather than a hand-drawn comparison. The figure is useful for forming a question, but not for proving the answer.

## The Missing Dots

The next question is not just where the points are, but **what the blank spaces actually mean**.

GeoNames gives me only **41 mine records and 2 ore treatment plants** for all of China. That looks extremely small beside China’s role in mineral production.

In 2024, China produced **99% of the world’s primary gallium, 82% of mined tungsten, 82% of natural graphite, and 71% of mined rare earths**. [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/china)

A 2026 USGS geospatial study gives an even clearer comparison. Its China mineral-industry database contains **932 mines and quarries and 1,506 mineral processing plants**. The categories are not identical to GeoNames, so the numbers cannot be compared one-to-one. Still, the difference shows how strongly the choice of dataset and classification system changes what becomes visible. [USGS China mineral-industry geospatial study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr20261018/full)

China’s own natural-resources data shows the same issue from another angle. The [2024 China Natural Resources Bulletin](https://www.cgs.gov.cn/zcwj/202503/t20250314_820792.html) reported **30,391 registered mining rights** at the end of 2024.

A mining right is a legal right or permit connected to mining, not one physical mine, so it cannot be compared directly with the 41 GeoNames points. Still, the difference in scale makes it clear that my GeoNames map captures only a small part of a much larger mining system.

This means I cannot look at an empty area on the map and simply say:

**there is no mining here.**

A blank area could mean there is no matching feature. But it could also mean that the place is classified under another code, was never added to GeoNames, has not been updated, or does not fit the exact feature definition I selected.

Some blank spaces may therefore reflect real geography.

Others may reflect the database.

That distinction matters.

This changes the question from:

**“Why is there nothing here?”**

to:

**“Why does nothing appear here under the categories I selected?”**

That is a much more careful way to read the map.

## Classification Can Make Places Disappear

The feature-code definitions make this even clearer.

`MN` refers specifically to a mine where mineral ore is extracted from the ground. A place could be important to the mining industry and still not count as `MN`.

`MLM` is narrower again. It refers specifically to a plant where ore is treated to increase its metal content. A refinery, smelter, quarry, factory, storage site, or other industrial facility may therefore be important to the mineral supply chain without appearing as `MLM`.

So some of my “missing dots” may not actually be missing places.

They may be places hidden by classification.

This matters because classification happens before I ever see the map. Someone has already decided what categories exist and what each category means.

My filter then adds another decision on top of that.

## A Dot Is Also a Simplification

There is another limitation that is easy to miss: **a mine is not really a dot**.

A mine can include pits, underground tunnels, processing buildings, roads, storage areas, waste areas, and other infrastructure spread across a large area. A port can stretch across kilometres of coastline and contain many separate terminals.

On my map, however, each location becomes one coordinate.

That point tells me roughly **where** GeoNames records the feature.

It does not tell me how large the site is, how much it produces, what mineral it handles, who owns it, whether it is currently active, how many people work there, or how important it is.

This also means that two dots can look equal even when the places they represent are completely different.

A small local port and one of the world’s largest ports can both appear as one blue circle.

A small mine and a major extraction site can both appear as one brown circle.

So even if every location were recorded perfectly, the map would still simplify the real industrial landscape.

## What the Dates Tell Me — and What They Do Not

I kept the **last updated date** in the popup because it gives the reader some information about the record itself.

According to the [GeoNames data format](https://download.geonames.org/export/dump/readme.txt), the modification date records the **date of the last modification** to that GeoNames entry.

That is useful, but it needs to be read carefully.

A recent modification date does not necessarily mean that someone recently visited the mine, port, or plant and confirmed that it is still operating. It only tells me that the GeoNames record was last changed on that date.

In the same way, an older modification date does not prove that a place has closed or disappeared. The record may simply not have needed an edit.

This means there is also a time problem in the map.

My external statistics mostly describe China in **2024 and 2025**, while individual GeoNames records may have been modified at very different times.

The fact that I downloaded the dataset in 2026 does not turn every point into a verified 2026 observation.

The map therefore brings together records created and updated at different times and displays them together as if they belong to one present moment.

That is another reason to be careful when treating the map as a complete picture of China today.

## GeoNames as a Data Assemblage

This connects closely to Kitchin and Lauriault’s idea of a **data assemblage**.

In [*Towards Critical Data Studies: Charting and Unpacking Data Assemblages and Their Work*](https://kitchin.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/towards-critical-data-studies-2018.pdf), Kitchin and Lauriault argue that data should not be treated as neutral information that simply exists on its own.

Data is produced through people, institutions, technologies, standards, categories, rules, infrastructure, and decisions.

That is exactly what happened before I even downloaded the China file.

Someone had to decide what counts as a mine, what counts as an ore treatment plant, which feature codes should exist, which locations should be added, what places should be called, how their coordinates should be stored, and when records should be changed.

GeoNames also combines information from different sources. Its [China page](https://www.geonames.org/countries/CN/) lists the **National Bureau of Statistics of China** and **Beijing City Lab** as national data providers. GeoNames also allows contributions and corrections from users.

This means there is no single person or institution creating “the China dataset.”

The dataset is assembled from different people, institutions, systems, and decisions.

There is also an important limit here. Knowing which institutions contribute to GeoNames does not necessarily tell me which source produced one specific mine, port, or treatment plant record.

I therefore know something about the provenance of GeoNames as a whole, but much less about the provenance of each individual point.

That matters because different institutions collect different types of information, at different times, and for different reasons.

One source may be good at administrative geography. Another may focus on population. Another may contribute local place names. That does not automatically produce equal coverage of mines, ports, factories, or processing facilities.

This is why Kitchin and Lauriault describe data as **situated, contingent, relational, and framed**.

The points are not simply “China.”

They are China as recorded through this particular data system.

## Then I Changed the Data Again

GeoNames made choices before I received the dataset, but I also made choices after downloading it.

I decided to select only `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT`.

That single filtering decision removed almost everything else in the China file.

I then chose the colours, point sizes, basemap, popup information, map scale, annotations, rivers, highlighted clusters, and which figures the reader would see.

There are therefore at least two layers of selection.

First, GeoNames decides what becomes data.

Then I decide what part of that data becomes visible.

These choices did not change the coordinates, but they changed what stood out.

Using blue for ports makes them visually connect with water.

Using brown for mines connects them visually with land.

Highlighting the Yangtze River makes nearby ports look more connected.

Showing 131 ports at the same time makes the port layer feel more important simply because it occupies more of the screen.

Changing marker size can also make one category feel more important even when size is not measuring real-world importance.

The map may therefore be technically correct while still guiding the viewer toward a particular interpretation.

## Why *Do Maps Lie?* Matters Here

The Sheffield Methods Institute’s [*Do Maps Lie?*](https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/education/smi/q-step/why-numbers-matter) helped me think about this beyond simple mistakes.

A map does not need to contain false information to be misleading.

The video uses London house-price data to show how the same underlying information can create different impressions depending on how it is grouped and displayed.

My map does something similar through selection and design rather than price categories.

If I show all three layers, the **131 port points dominate**.

If I hide the ports, the inland mine pattern becomes much easier to see.

If I add rivers, the ports begin to look connected.

If I add terrain, the mines begin to look related to elevation.

If I make one marker larger, it becomes easier to notice even if its real-world importance has not changed.

The coordinates stay the same.

**The story does not.**

That is the part of *Do Maps Lie?* that matters most for my project.

The problem is not always that a map contains something false.

Sometimes the problem is that a map makes one interpretation feel obvious.

My port figure can make proximity look like connection.

My terrain figure can make two patterns look causal.

A blank area can look like proof that nothing exists there.

A single dot can look like a complete representation of a huge industrial site.

None of those conclusions are automatically contained in the original coordinates.

They come from the way the data is selected, combined, and shown.

This does not mean maps are useless or that every map is lying.

It means maps need to be read as arguments, not just pictures.

The person making the map decides what enters the frame, what disappears, what is emphasised, and what relationships are made easier to see.

In this case, that person is also me.

## A Note on the Coding and Formatting

Most of the changes I made to the original notebook came from thinking about what would make sense to someone clicking on the map for the first time.

The original feature codes were not very intuitive. `PRT` is somewhat easy to guess, but `MN` and especially `MLM` are much less clear. I changed the popup so the reader sees the full feature type instead of only the code.

I also changed the colours so each category has a simple visual logic. <span style="color:#9A6B3F;">Brown represents mines</span> because it connects visually to land and extraction. <span style="color:#2A9D8F;">Teal represents ore treatment plants</span> because it feels more industrial. <span style="color:#4361EE;">Blue represents ports</span> because it connects naturally to water and movement.

The feature type inside each popup uses the same colour as the point so the popup and map feel connected.

I also experimented with adding province names through a separate lookup table. The lookup introduced incorrect labels, and the province information did not add enough to justify another manually created layer of data, so I removed it.

That was a useful reminder that adding more information does not automatically improve a dataset. If I could not trust the province labels, leaving them out was better than presenting them as correct.

The final popup therefore keeps only the information I actually use: **name, feature type, latitude, longitude, and last updated date**.

## Where I Could Use This Again

This project connects directly to my capstone on international law and critical minerals. I was hoping it would give me more direction on how I could study critical mineral supply chains, and I think it did.

At first, I thought mapping mines, processing plants, and ports would give me a fairly clear picture of how materials move from extraction to export.

It did not.

Instead, it showed me what would actually be needed to build that picture properly.

A larger project could combine mines, processing plants, ports, railways, roads, shipping routes, companies, ownership records, mineral types, production volumes, trade flows, and the laws or regulations that govern them.

Only then could I begin to show whether a mine, processing plant, and port actually belong to the same supply chain.

This is also important from a policymaking perspective.

Maps can make large amounts of information much easier to understand. They can help show where activity is concentrated, where important infrastructure is located, and where possible gaps or risks may exist.

But policymakers also need to be careful about what a map seems to show.

A mine, processing plant, and port may appear close together, but **proximity does not prove causality or even connection**.

One did not necessarily cause another to be located there.

They may not belong to the same company.

They may not handle the same mineral.

They may not even be part of the same supply chain.

Proving those relationships would require other evidence, such as trade data, ownership records, company information, transport routes, and legal documents.

By the end of the project, I realized that my map is much better at **generating questions** than proving relationships.

At the beginning, I thought I was asking:

**Where are China’s mines, processing plants, and ports?**

A more accurate question is:

**Where does GeoNames record places classified as `MN`, `MLM`, and `PRT`, and what patterns appear when I map those records?**

That sounds like a small change, but it completely changes what I can claim.

The map still shows useful patterns. It shows major coastal port concentrations, inland mine records, two very different ore treatment plant locations, and several relationships worth investigating.

But it does not show the full supply chain.

And that is probably the most useful thing I learned from the assignment.
